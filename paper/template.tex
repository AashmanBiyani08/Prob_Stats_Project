\title{Statistical Modelling of Packet-Loss Patterns in Computer Networks: Distribution Fitting, Burstiness Testing and Bayesian Estimation}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{BAMAT207 Probability and Statistics: Case Study Report}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The loss rate of a network is usually reported as a single number, and the simplest models assume that each packet is lost independently of the others. If that held, the number of losses per second would be Poisson and the gap from one loss to the next would be exponential. In this study we check how much of this survives when losses arrive in bursts. Three networks (wired broadband, campus Wi-Fi and a mobile hotspot) were simulated with a two-state Gilbert-Elliott model, each sending <<rate>> packets per second for <<duration_h>> hour, which comes to <<packets>> packets per network. We fitted models for the counts and for the waiting times, tested whether neighbouring packets are independent, and estimated the loss rates with a Bayesian Beta-Binomial model. Independence did not hold on any of the <<n_scen>> networks. The variance of the per-second loss count was <<list_D>> times the mean, where a Poisson model gives 1, and after a single loss the next packet was <<amp_lo>> to <<amp_hi>> times more likely to be lost. This shows up directly in the error bars. Over <<cov_reps>> simulated captures, the standard 95\% interval for the loss rate contained the true value in only <<covN_lo>> to <<covN_hi>> of cases. Reducing the sample size by a design effect raised coverage to between <<covA_lo>> and <<covA_hi>>. A power study suggests recording at least ten minutes on each network once we move on to real measurements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Most people have run into packet loss without calling it that. A video call on poor Wi-Fi freezes for a second, or a word drops out of the audio, and then the call picks up again. Underneath, the application is sending small packets at a fixed rate, and a few of them never reach the other end. The fraction that goes missing is called the loss rate. It is the figure written into service agreements, and protocol designers rely on it when they choose retransmission timers or decide how much error correction a stream should carry.

What the loss rate does not show is how the losses are spread out in time. If ten packets are lost at scattered points over a minute, the decoder can hide each gap and the user hardly notices. Ten losses in a row produce an obvious freeze. Textbook models usually sidestep this by assuming that every packet is lost independently with the same small probability $p$. The assumption is convenient: it makes the number of losses in a short window Binomial, and so close to Poisson, and the time between losses exponential. Once losses start to cluster, neither result is correct, and the error bars worked out from them are wrong too.

Our aim was to find out how badly independence fails, which standard tests pick up the failure, and what it does to the interval estimates usually reported for the loss rate. The work is split into two phases, and this report covers Phase 1, a simulation study. Because we set the true parameters of each network ourselves, we can compare every estimate and test with the correct answer before the same code is used on real data. Phase 2 will apply that code to real captures.

The report is organised as follows. Section~II gives some background, Section~III sets out the loss model and how the data were generated, Section~IV describes the statistical methods and Section~V reports the results. Section~VI discusses what the results mean and where the study is limited, and Section~VII concludes.

\section{Background}
Modelling bursty errors with a hidden two-state chain goes back to E.~N.~Gilbert, who used it for errors on telephone lines in 1960. In 1963 E.~O.~Elliott extended the model so that errors could occur in either state. The Gilbert-Elliott model that came out of this is still the usual starting point for bursty loss.

It has also been known for many years that Internet losses are not independent. Measurement studies in the 1990s showed that probe packets sent close together were often lost together, and that losses in TCP transfers came in correlated episodes. The timing of loss in multicast traces could be described reasonably well with Markov chains. Later studies fitted Gilbert models to voice traffic and related the length of a loss burst to how poor a call sounds.

Most of this earlier work sets out to describe the loss process. Our interest is more statistical. We want to know which standard tests detect the dependence, how much data they need before they do, and how far ordinary interval estimates drift from the truth when the dependence is ignored. For the last of these we use the design effect from survey sampling, where it corrects for people being sampled in clusters instead of one at a time.

\section{Loss Model and Data}
\subsection{The Gilbert-Elliott Channel}
We treat the network as being in one of two hidden states, Good (G) or Bad (B). We cannot observe the state itself, only what it does to the packets. Before each packet, the channel switches from Good to Bad with probability $p$ and from Bad back to Good with probability $r$. In the Good state a packet is lost with a small probability $h_G$; in the Bad state the probability $h_B$ is much larger. Over a long run, the flow into the Bad state has to equal the flow out of it. This gives the share of time spent in Bad, and from it the overall loss rate:
\begin{equation}
\pi_B=\frac{p}{p+r},\qquad P(\text{loss})=(1-\pi_B)\,h_G+\pi_B\,h_B .
\label{eq:ge}
\end{equation}
The number of packets spent in a state before leaving it is geometric, with a mean of $1/p$ packets for Good and $1/r$ for Bad. A burst of losses happens whenever the channel drops into the Bad state for a few packets.

\subsection{Simulation}
The chain could be simulated by stepping forward one packet at a time, but that is slow. Instead, we draw the length of each stay in Good or Bad directly from the geometric distribution and place the stays one after another. Then, for every packet, we decide whether it is lost using the loss probability of the state it falls in. The process is the same, just far quicker to generate. We take four things from each trace: the number of losses in each <<window_s>>-s window (<<windows>> counts per network), the gaps between successive losses, the lengths of bursts (runs of consecutive losses), and how often a delivered (N) or lost (L) packet is followed by each type of packet. Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline} plots the first few minutes of each trace. On Wi-Fi and the mobile hotspot the clumping can already be seen by eye.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Fitting the Distributions}
For the per-window loss counts $x_1,\dots,x_n$, the first model we fit is the Poisson distribution, whose maximum-likelihood estimate is simply the mean count, $\hat\lambda=\bar x$. The natural alternative is the Negative Binomial:
\begin{align}
P(X=k)&=\binom{k+r_{\!NB}-1}{k}q^{r_{\!NB}}(1-q)^k,\\
\operatorname{Var}(X)&=\mu+\mu^2/r_{\!NB} .
\end{align}
The Negative Binomial can be thought of as a Poisson whose rate changes over time, which is what a network moving between good and bad periods produces. The extra term $\mu^2/r_{\!NB}$ lets the variance exceed the mean, something the Poisson cannot do. We fit it by fixing the mean at $\bar x$ and searching for the $r_{\!NB}$ that maximises the likelihood.

For the gaps between losses we begin with the exponential distribution, with $\hat\lambda=1/\bar t$, and compare it with a mixture of two exponentials known as the two-phase hyperexponential:
\begin{equation}
f(t)=w\lambda_1e^{-\lambda_1t}+(1-w)\lambda_2e^{-\lambda_2t}.
\end{equation}
The idea behind the mixture is that gaps come in two types. Short gaps, with the fast rate $\lambda_1$, occur within a burst. Long gaps, with the slow rate $\lambda_2$, occur between bursts. We never know which type a particular gap is, so we fit the mixture with the Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm. In each iteration the E-step works out the probability that each gap is a short one, and the M-step updates $w$, $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ with these probabilities as weights. We stop once the fit no longer improves. Models are compared with the Akaike Information Criterion, $\text{AIC}=2k-2\ln L$. It rewards a good fit but adds a penalty for each extra parameter, so a lower value means a better model.

\subsection{Goodness of Fit}
We check the count models by comparing the observed number of windows with each count against the number the model expects, using the Pearson statistic $\chi^2=\sum_i (O_i-E_i)^2/E_i$. The test is unreliable when expected counts in the tail are small, so tail bins are merged until every expected count is at least 5. The degrees of freedom are the number of bins, minus one, minus the number of fitted parameters. We also apply the index-of-dispersion test. If the counts are Poisson, $(n-1)s^2/\bar x$ follows a $\chi^2_{n-1}$ distribution, so a variance well above the mean gives a very large value of the statistic.

For the gaps we use the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) distance $D=\sup_t|F_n(t)-F(t)|$, the largest vertical distance between the cumulative curve of the data and that of the model. One complication is that the standard KS tables assume the model was fixed before the data were seen. Here the rate is estimated from the same data, which makes the fit look better than it is and leaves the textbook p-value too large. We get the p-value from a parametric bootstrap instead. A synthetic data set is simulated from the fitted model, the model is refitted, and the new $D$ is recorded. Doing this <<n_boot>> times for the exponential and <<n_boot_mix>> times for the mixture shows the range of $D$ to expect if the model were in fact correct.

\subsection{Testing Independence}
The Wald-Wolfowitz runs test works on the 0/1 sequence of delivered and lost packets and counts the number of runs $R$, a run being a stretch of identical outcomes. With $n_1$ losses and $n_0$ deliveries, independence gives
\begin{equation}
E[R]=\frac{2n_1n_0}{n}+1,\quad \operatorname{Var}(R)=\frac{2n_1n_0(2n_1n_0-n)}{n^2(n-1)} .
\end{equation}
When losses tend to occur together, the sequence switches between lost and delivered less often than it would by chance, and $R$ ends up smaller than expected.

The Markov-chain test asks the question more directly: does the outcome for one packet depend on the packet before it? Under $H_0$ all packets share a single loss probability. Under $H_1$ there are two, $a=P(L\mid N)$ after a delivered packet and $b=P(L\mid L)$ after a lost one. Writing $n_{xy}$ for the number of times outcome $x$ is followed by outcome $y$,
\begin{equation}
\hat a=\frac{n_{NL}}{n_{NN}+n_{NL}},\qquad \hat b=\frac{n_{LL}}{n_{LN}+n_{LL}},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
G^2=2(\ln L_1-\ln L_0).
\end{equation}
Because $H_1$ has one parameter more than $H_0$, Wilks' theorem says that $G^2$ is approximately $\chi^2_1$ when $H_0$ holds. The two estimates are useful on their own as well. The ratio $b/a$ measures how much a loss raises the chance of the next loss, and $1/(1-b)$ gives the mean burst length.

The three networks are compared with rank-based tests, since the counts are mostly zero, strongly skewed and far from normal. The Kruskal-Wallis test compares all three networks together. Pairwise Mann-Whitney tests compare them two at a time, with a Bonferroni correction so that running three tests does not push up the false-alarm rate. A $\chi^2$ test of homogeneity on the totals of lost and delivered packets checks whether the loss proportion is the same on every network. All tests use $\alpha=<<alpha>>$.

\subsection{Bayesian Estimation}
We estimate the loss rate itself the Bayesian way. The prior is a flat $\text{Beta}(1,1)$, which treats every loss rate between 0 and 1 as equally likely before any data are seen. After $k$ losses in $n$ packets the posterior becomes $\text{Beta}(1+k,\,1+n-k)$, so updating just means adding the observed losses and deliveries to the prior counts. We report the posterior mean with the 95\% credible interval, and for <<seq_label>> we also show how the posterior narrows as more traffic is observed. To check whether the networks really are ordered by loss rate, we draw <<ord_draws>> values from each posterior and count the share of draws in which Wired $<$ Wi-Fi $<$ Mobile.

The burst parameters are handled the same way. We place separate posteriors on $a$ and $b$, $a\sim\text{Beta}(1+n_{NL},1+n_{NN})$ and $b\sim\text{Beta}(1+n_{LL},1+n_{LN})$, and these give credible intervals for $b/a$ and for the burst length. As a check on the independent model, we also use it to predict how often a window should contain at least <<heavy_k>> losses, and compare that with how often it actually happens.

\subsection{Design Effect}
The variance of a sum shows why bursts cause problems for interval estimates:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_i X_i\Big)=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(X_i)+2\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j).
\end{equation}
For independent packets every covariance term is zero, and the variance of the loss count is just $np(1-p)$. Within a burst, though, neighbouring packets tend to have the same outcome. The covariances are positive and the true variance is larger. The standard interval ignores this and comes out too narrow.

To correct for it, we divide each trace into blocks of $m=<<block_packets>>$ packets (<<block_s>>~s) and compare the observed spread of the block counts with the spread independence would give:
\begin{equation}
\text{deff}=\frac{s^2_{\text{block}}}{m\,\hat p(1-\hat p)},\qquad n_{\text{eff}}=\frac{n}{\text{deff}},
\end{equation}
where deff is not allowed to fall below 1. You can read $n_{\text{eff}}$ as the number of independent packets that carry the same information as our correlated ones. The adjusted posterior uses these effective counts, $\text{Beta}(1+k/\text{deff},\,1+(n-k)/\text{deff})$, and the interval it gives is wider and closer to the truth.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Checks}
Two of our questions cannot be answered from a single trace, so we answer them by repeating the experiment many times. The first is power, the probability that each test notices the bursts. For capture lengths between <<dur_lo>>~s and <<dur_hi>>~s, we simulate <<power_reps>> captures per network and record how often each test rejects independence. The second is coverage: how often a 95\% interval actually includes the true loss rate. For this we simulate <<cov_reps>> captures of <<cov_min>> minutes for each network, build the naive and the adjusted interval for every capture, and count how many contain the true value. With <<power_reps>> repetitions, each of these proportions has a standard error of about <<mc_se>>.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
It takes surprisingly little for independence to fail. A Bad spell of only a few packets is enough to break every model built on that assumption, and the loss rate alone gives no hint of it. Mobile loses roughly <<ratio_MW>> times as many packets as Wired, yet on both networks a loss makes another loss tens of times more likely. Burstiness is a separate property of a link and has to be measured separately.

The coverage results show the problem is bigger than picking the right model. A naive interval counts every packet as a new and independent piece of evidence. When packets within a burst share the same outcome, there is much less independent evidence than the packet count suggests, and the interval turns out too narrow. The effect grows as the network becomes burstier, which is why Mobile has the lowest naive coverage. The design-effect correction is simple, needs only block counts, and brings coverage close to 95\% on all three networks.

There are limits to what we can claim. The main one is that the data are simulated, and from the same kind of model that motivated the study. The tests do not use this, and the true parameters are only used to judge the estimates, but real links have other effects, such as busy hours, that our model leaves out. Phase 2 is meant to deal with this.

There are also a few smaller points. The dispersion test compares the counts with a Poisson distribution, but counts out of <<rate>> packets are really Binomial, with a variance slightly below the mean. This makes the test a little conservative, so it is more likely to understate burstiness than to overstate it. The runs and Markov tests rely on large-sample approximations. In very short captures their false-alarm rate may drift from 5\%, although for hour-long traces it stays close to 5\%. The Negative Binomial and the two-phase mixture are both still rejected on Wi-Fi and Mobile, so neither gives a complete description. The design effect depends on the block length we choose, and our <<block_s>>-s blocks are much longer than any Bad spell, which should be enough. Finally, the headline figures come from one seeded trace per network, although the Monte Carlo checks repeat the whole experiment thousands of times.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We tested whether packet losses on <<n_scen>> simulated networks behave independently, and found that they do not. The Poisson and exponential models were rejected in every case. The per-second loss counts were <<list_D>> times more spread out than a Poisson model allows, the gaps split into short gaps within bursts and long quiet gaps between them, and each loss made the next one <<amp_lo>> to <<amp_hi>> times more likely. Most importantly, the standard 95\% interval for the loss rate was right in only <<covN_lo>> to <<covN_hi>> of cases. Dividing the sample size by the design effect raised this to between <<covA_lo>> and <<covA_hi>>.

The next step is Phase 2. We will record steady streams of <<rate>> packets per second on real wired, Wi-Fi and mobile links, for at least ten minutes each, and run the same code on them. After that we would like to estimate the Gilbert-Elliott parameters directly from the real traces using the Baum-Welch algorithm, try richer models for the tail, use the Phase 1 results as priors in a hierarchical Bayesian model across the networks, and compare loss at peak and off-peak hours.
