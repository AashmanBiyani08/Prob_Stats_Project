\title{Statistical Modelling of Packet-Loss Patterns in Computer Networks: Distribution Fitting, Burstiness Testing and Bayesian Estimation}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{BAMAT207 Probability and Statistics: Case Study Report}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Loss rate is normally reported as one number, and the easiest models treat every lost packet as if it had nothing to do with the packets around it. Under that view, losses per second are Poisson and the waiting time between losses is exponential. We wanted to know what happens to these results when losses come in bursts instead. To find out, we simulated <<n_scen>> networks (wired broadband, campus Wi-Fi and a mobile hotspot) using a two-state Gilbert-Elliott model, with each one sending <<rate>> packets a second for <<duration_h>> hour, or <<packets>> packets in all. On these traces we fitted models for the counts and for the gaps, tested whether neighbouring packets are independent, and estimated loss rates with a Beta-Binomial model. Independence broke down on all three. The variance of the per-second count came out at <<list_D>> times the mean (Poisson would give 1), and once a packet was lost, the next one was <<amp_lo>> to <<amp_hi>> times more likely to go too. The practical cost showed up in the error bars. In <<cov_reps>> simulated captures, the usual 95\% interval for the loss rate held the true value only <<covN_lo>> to <<covN_hi>> of the time. Cutting the sample size down by a design effect brought that back up to <<covA_lo>> to <<covA_hi>>. A power study suggests recording at least ten minutes on each network once we start taking real measurements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Most of us have run into packet loss without giving it a name. A video call on bad Wi-Fi stalls for a second, a word goes missing from the audio, and then things pick up again. What is happening underneath is simple enough. The app sends small packets at a steady rate and a few of them never make it. The fraction that goes missing is the loss rate. It shows up in service agreements, and it is what protocol designers look at when they tune retransmission timers or pick how much error correction to add.

The catch is that a loss rate says how many packets were lost but not when. Lose ten packets scattered across a minute and the decoder quietly fills each hole; nobody notices. Lose ten in a row and the picture freezes. The standard textbook move is to assume that every packet is lost on its own, with the same small probability $p$. It is a convenient assumption because it hands you two clean results: the number of losses in a short window is Binomial (so close to Poisson), and the time between losses is exponential. If losses cluster, neither result holds, and any error bars built on top of them are off as well.

So we asked three things. How far does independence actually break? Which of the usual tests notice? And what happens to the interval people quote for the loss rate? The work is planned in two phases, and this report is about the first one, a simulation study. Since we picked every network's true parameters ourselves, we always know the right answer, which lets us check each estimate and each test before we point the same code at real data in Phase 2.

Section~II gives some background. Section~III describes the loss model and how we generated the data, Section~IV goes through the statistical methods, and Section~V has the results. We discuss what they mean, and where the study falls short, in Section~VI, and wrap up in Section~VII.

\section{Background}
Using a hidden two-state chain for bursty errors is an old idea. E.~N.~Gilbert proposed it in 1960 for errors on telephone lines, and E.~O.~Elliott generalised it in 1963 so that either state could produce errors. More than sixty years later, the Gilbert-Elliott model is still where most people start when they want to describe bursty loss.

Nor is it news that Internet losses are not independent. Measurement work in the 1990s found that probe packets sent close together were often lost together, that TCP transfers lost packets in correlated episodes, and that Markov chains did a decent job of describing loss timing in multicast traces. Later studies fitted Gilbert models to voice traffic and tied burst length to how bad a call sounds.

That earlier work mostly set out to describe the loss process. We come at it from the statistics side instead. Which standard tests pick up the dependence, how much data do they need, and how far off are the usual intervals if you ignore it? For that last question we borrowed the design effect from survey sampling. There it is used when people are sampled in clusters, say whole households, rather than one by one, which turns out to be a close match for packets lost in bursts.

\section{Loss Model and Data}
\subsection{The Gilbert-Elliott Channel}
It helps to think of the network as having two moods, Good (G) and Bad (B). We never see the mood itself, only what it does to the packets. Before each packet the channel can switch: Good to Bad with probability $p$, Bad to Good with probability $r$. In Good, a packet is lost with a small probability $h_G$. In Bad, the loss probability $h_B$ is much higher. Over a long run, the channel has to enter Bad as often as it leaves, and that balance gives the share of time spent in Bad and, from it, the overall loss rate:
\begin{equation}
\pi_B=\frac{p}{p+r},\qquad P(\text{loss})=(1-\pi_B)\,h_G+\pi_B\,h_B .
\label{eq:ge}
\end{equation}
How long the channel stays in a state is geometric, with an average of $1/p$ packets in Good and $1/r$ in Bad. A burst is just what happens when the channel drops into Bad for a handful of packets.

\subsection{Simulation}
The obvious way to simulate the chain is to step it forward one packet at a time. That works, but it is slow. What we do instead is draw the length of each Good or Bad stay straight from the geometric distribution, place the stays one after another, and only then go through the packets and decide which were lost, using the loss probability of whichever state each packet landed in. The process is exactly the same; it just runs much faster. From every trace we take out four things: the loss count in each <<window_s>>-s window (<<windows>> per network), the gaps between losses, the burst lengths (runs of back-to-back losses), and how often a delivered (N) or a lost (L) packet is followed by each kind. Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline} plots the first few minutes. You do not need any statistics to see that Wi-Fi and mobile losses come in clumps.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Fitting the Distributions}
For the per-window loss counts $x_1,\dots,x_n$, the natural first model is a Poisson, and its maximum-likelihood estimate is just the average count, $\hat\lambda=\bar x$. The natural second model is the Negative Binomial:
\begin{align}
P(X=k)&=\binom{k+r_{\!NB}-1}{k}q^{r_{\!NB}}(1-q)^k,\\
\operatorname{Var}(X)&=\mu+\mu^2/r_{\!NB} .
\end{align}
A handy way to think of the Negative Binomial is as a Poisson whose rate does not sit still, which is what you get when a network keeps switching between good and bad stretches. The $\mu^2/r_{\!NB}$ term is what lets the variance climb above the mean, something a Poisson simply cannot do. We fit it by fixing the mean at $\bar x$ and searching over $r_{\!NB}$ for the highest likelihood.

For the gaps between losses, the exponential is the natural first model, with $\hat\lambda=1/\bar t$. Our alternative mixes two exponentials together, which is known as a two-phase hyperexponential:
\begin{equation}
f(t)=w\lambda_1e^{-\lambda_1t}+(1-w)\lambda_2e^{-\lambda_2t}.
\end{equation}
The thinking behind it is that gaps come in two kinds. Short gaps, with the fast rate $\lambda_1$, fall inside a burst. Long gaps, with the slow rate $\lambda_2$, are the quiet stretches between bursts. The problem is that no gap comes labelled, so we fit the mixture with the Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm. Each round has two steps: first estimate, for every gap, how likely it is to be a short one; then refit $w$, $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ with those likelihoods as weights. We keep going until the fit stops getting better. Models are compared using the Akaike Information Criterion, $\text{AIC}=2k-2\ln L$. It rewards a good fit but charges a penalty for each extra parameter, and the lower score wins.

\subsection{Goodness of Fit}
For the count models, we line up how many windows actually had each count against how many the model predicts and use the Pearson statistic $\chi^2=\sum_i (O_i-E_i)^2/E_i$. The test gets shaky when expected counts are tiny, which happens in the tail, so we merge tail bins until every expected count is at least 5. Degrees of freedom are the number of bins, less one, less the number of fitted parameters. We also run the index-of-dispersion test. For Poisson counts, $(n-1)s^2/\bar x$ follows $\chi^2_{n-1}$, so a variance well above the mean shows up as a huge statistic.

For the gaps we use the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) distance $D=\sup_t|F_n(t)-F(t)|$, the biggest vertical gap between the data's cumulative curve and the model's. There is a trap here that is easy to miss. The usual KS tables assume the model was fixed before you looked at the data. We estimate the rate from the very data we are testing, which makes the fit look better than it is and the p-value too large. Our fix is a parametric bootstrap: simulate a fake data set from the fitted model, refit it, note its $D$, and repeat, <<n_boot>> times for the exponential and <<n_boot_mix>> for the mixture. That shows how big $D$ normally gets when the model really is right.

\subsection{Testing Independence}
The Wald-Wolfowitz runs test treats the trace as a string of 0s and 1s (delivered or lost) and counts the runs $R$, the unbroken stretches of the same outcome. With $n_1$ losses and $n_0$ deliveries, independence predicts
\begin{equation}
E[R]=\frac{2n_1n_0}{n}+1,\quad \operatorname{Var}(R)=\frac{2n_1n_0(2n_1n_0-n)}{n^2(n-1)} .
\end{equation}
When losses stick together, the string flips between lost and delivered less often than chance would, so $R$ comes out low.

The Markov-chain test gets at the question more directly. Does what happened to one packet change the odds for the next? Under $H_0$, no: every packet has the same loss probability. Under $H_1$, yes: there is one probability after a delivered packet, $a=P(L\mid N)$, and another after a lost one, $b=P(L\mid L)$. Writing $n_{xy}$ for the number of times outcome $x$ is followed by outcome $y$,
\begin{equation}
\hat a=\frac{n_{NL}}{n_{NN}+n_{NL}},\qquad \hat b=\frac{n_{LL}}{n_{LN}+n_{LL}},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
G^2=2(\ln L_1-\ln L_0).
\end{equation}
Because $H_1$ has one parameter more than $H_0$, Wilks' theorem tells us $G^2$ is roughly $\chi^2_1$ when $H_0$ holds. The two estimates are worth keeping for their own sake, too. The ratio $b/a$ says how much a loss raises the odds of another, and $1/(1-b)$ is the average burst length.

To compare the three networks we stuck to rank-based tests. The counts are mostly zeros and heavily skewed, so anything that assumes normality would be on thin ice. Kruskal-Wallis compares all three together; pairwise Mann-Whitney tests compare them two at a time, with a Bonferroni correction so that running three tests does not quietly push up the false-alarm rate. A $\chi^2$ test of homogeneity on the lost and delivered totals checks whether the loss proportion is the same on every network. All tests use $\alpha=<<alpha>>$.

\subsection{Bayesian Estimation}
For the loss rate itself we went Bayesian. We start from a flat $\text{Beta}(1,1)$ prior, meaning that before seeing any data we treat every loss rate between 0 and 1 as equally likely. After $k$ losses in $n$ packets the posterior is $\text{Beta}(1+k,\,1+n-k)$, so in practice we just add the observed losses and deliveries to the prior. We report the posterior mean with a 95\% credible interval, and for <<seq_label>> we also show how the posterior tightens as traffic comes in. To check that the networks really are ranked by loss rate, we draw <<ord_draws>> values from each posterior and count how often Wired $<$ Wi-Fi $<$ Mobile comes out.

The burst parameters get the same treatment. Each has its own posterior, $a\sim\text{Beta}(1+n_{NL},1+n_{NN})$ and $b\sim\text{Beta}(1+n_{LL},1+n_{LN})$, and from these we get credible intervals for $b/a$ and for the burst length. As a sanity check on the independent model, we also ask it how often a window should hold at least <<heavy_k>> losses, and set that next to how often it really does.

\subsection{Design Effect}
Why bursts are bad news for interval estimates becomes clear from the variance of a sum:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_i X_i\Big)=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(X_i)+2\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j).
\end{equation}
If the packets were independent, every covariance would be zero and the variance of the loss count would be $np(1-p)$. Inside a burst, though, neighbouring packets tend to go the same way, so the covariances are positive and the true variance is bigger. The textbook interval leaves this out, so it is too narrow.

To fix it, we cut each trace into blocks of $m=<<block_packets>>$ packets (<<block_s>>~s) and compare how much the block counts really vary with how much they would vary under independence:
\begin{equation}
\text{deff}=\frac{s^2_{\text{block}}}{m\,\hat p(1-\hat p)},\qquad n_{\text{eff}}=\frac{n}{\text{deff}},
\end{equation}
(deff is never allowed below 1.) One way to read $n_{\text{eff}}$ is as the number of independent packets that our correlated ones are worth. The adjusted posterior plugs in these effective counts, $\text{Beta}(1+k/\text{deff},\,1+(n-k)/\text{deff})$, and the interval comes out wider and more honest.

\subsection{Monte Carlo Checks}
Some questions cannot be settled from one trace, so we repeat the whole experiment many times. The first is power, or how likely each test is to spot the bursts. For capture lengths from <<dur_lo>>~s up to <<dur_hi>>~s we simulate <<power_reps>> captures per network and count how often each test rejects independence. The second is coverage, or how often a 95\% interval actually contains the true loss rate. Here we simulate <<cov_reps>> captures of <<cov_min>> minutes per network, build both the naive and the adjusted interval for each one, and count the hits. With <<power_reps>> repeats, each proportion is good to within about <<mc_se>> (one standard error).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The striking thing is how little it takes. A Bad spell of a few packets is enough to break every model that assumes independence, and the loss rate on its own gives no hint of it. Mobile loses about <<ratio_MW>> times as many packets as Wired, yet on both of them one loss makes the next tens of times more likely. Burstiness is its own property of a link and needs to be measured on its own.

The coverage results take this past model choice. A naive interval treats every packet as a new, independent piece of evidence. When packets in a burst share the same fate, there is much less independent evidence than the packet count suggests, and the interval ends up too tight. The burstier the network, the worse this gets, which is why Mobile has the lowest naive coverage. The design-effect fix is cheap (all it needs is block counts) and brings coverage close to 95\% on all three networks.

There are clear limits to what we can claim. The biggest one is that the data are simulated, and from the very kind of model that motivated the study. The tests do not use this, and we only use the true parameters to judge the estimates, but real links have effects our model leaves out, busy hours being the obvious one. That is what Phase 2 is for.

A few smaller points. The dispersion test compares the counts with a Poisson, but counts out of <<rate>> packets are strictly Binomial, with a variance a little under the mean. That makes the test slightly conservative, so if anything it understates burstiness. The runs and Markov tests rely on large-sample approximations. In very short captures their false-alarm rate can move away from 5\%, though on hour-long traces it stays close. The Negative Binomial and the two-phase mixture are both still rejected on Wi-Fi and Mobile, so neither is the full story. The design effect depends on the block length; ours is <<block_s>>~s, much longer than any Bad spell, which should be enough. Last, the headline numbers come from a single seeded trace per network, although the Monte Carlo checks repeat the whole experiment thousands of times.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We set out to test whether packet losses on <<n_scen>> simulated networks behave independently. They do not. Poisson and exponential models were rejected everywhere. Losses per second were <<list_D>> times more spread out than a Poisson allows, the gaps split into short in-burst gaps and long quiet ones, and one loss made the next <<amp_lo>> to <<amp_hi>> times more likely. The finding we would stress most is about the error bars: the usual 95\% interval for the loss rate was right only <<covN_lo>> to <<covN_hi>> of the time, and dividing the sample size by the design effect lifted that to <<covA_lo>> to <<covA_hi>>.

Phase 2 comes next. We will record steady streams of <<rate>> packets a second on real wired, Wi-Fi and mobile links, at least ten minutes each, and run the same code on them. After that, there are a few things we would like to try: estimating the Gilbert-Elliott parameters straight from the real traces with the Baum-Welch algorithm, fitting richer models to the tail, using the Phase 1 results as priors in a hierarchical Bayesian model that pools the networks, and comparing loss at peak and off-peak hours.
